Three decisions, and why

Everything below follows from these. Change one and the tables recompute; change none and the plans stand as priced.

  1. 01 The credit is redefined to 5,000 tokens, up from the 1,000 used in the cost analysis. That is arbitrary in cost terms and deliberate in product terms: it makes one tender ingest land on ≈100 credits, which a buyer can hold in their head. A unit nobody can reason about gets ignored, and then overage feels like a penalty rather than a meter.
  2. 02 Credits carry a 3–5× markup, not 10×. They are a fairness meter, not the profit centre — they exist so a heavy user is not subsidised by nine quiet ones. A punitive credit price teaches customers to ingest fewer documents, which is precisely the behaviour that makes the product stick.
  3. 03 The seat carries the margin. A seat costs about $2 a month to serve and is priced at $50–75. That is where the predictable revenue lives, and it is defensible because what a seat buys is access to the whole workflow, not a quantity of compute.

Why the included credits rise faster than the seats

Starter covers 2.1 tenders per seat per month, Scale covers 3.2. That is intentional: larger accounts run bigger tender books per person, and a bundle that ran out early would push them onto top-up rates and make the upgrade feel like a downgrade. The margin holds because the platform fee scales with the seat count regardless.

Note the deliberate asymmetry

An added seat is priced at $75 while a seat inside the Starter bundle is $50. Buying the next plan up is therefore always cheaper per seat than bolting seats onto the current one, which is what makes accounts upgrade instead of sprawling. If that friction proves too high in practice, drop the add-on to $60 before touching the plan prices.

The model multiplier is the safety valve

Because credits are defined against Sonnet 5, moving a customer to Opus 5 charges them ×1.67 credits for the same work rather than quietly eating the cost. The row above is the pessimistic case where you absorb it as a platform default; if Opus is offered as a customer-selectable quality tier, margin is unchanged and the 73% never happens.

The honest caveat

This is cost-plus, and cost-plus is the wrong axis here

Every number on this page is anchored to what the work costs to serve. That produces healthy 70–84% margins and a model that cannot lose money. It also prices a tender comparison at $11.82 — against 16 to 40 hours of engineer time that the comparison replaces.

The ratio of value delivered to price charged, at three plausible loaded rates for a procurement or proposals engineer.
Manual effort replacedLoaded cost of that effortChargedRatio

A 61× to 254× gap is not a rounding error in the assumptions — it is the signal that the constraint on price is what a buyer will agree to, not what the compute costs. Use these tiers as the floor and the internal margin model. Treat the list price as a separate decision to make once you have a signed customer and a number they said yes to.

  • The credit rate is close to right at 3–5×. Raising it further attacks the wrong line and discourages ingestion.
  • The seat price has the most headroom. $50–75 per seat is conservative for vertical B2B procurement software; the marginal cost is $2 either way.
  • Nothing here can be billed yet. No token usage is recorded anywhere in the codebase, so the meter these plans depend on does not exist. That remains the first thing to build.
