Running the pricing conversation

When to name a number, what to say when they push, and where the floor actually sits.

The sequence

Price is slide 13 of 19. Everything before it exists to make the number feel small. Do not let the conversation jump ahead — if they ask "what does it cost" on slide 3, answer with a question.

  1. 01
    Make them say the number first
    Before slide 13, get them to price the problem in their own words. Their figure is worth ten of ours because they cannot argue with it later.
    Roughly how long does one bid package take your team, start to finish? And who is doing that reading?
  2. 02
    Convert their answer to a year
    Days per tender, times tenders per year, times a loaded rate. Do the arithmetic out loud and let it land before you move on.
    So call it three days a tender, twenty tenders a month across the team — that is most of an engineer's year spent reading.
  3. 03
    Ask for the pilot, not the contract
    Name $7,500 plainly, once, and then stop talking. The credit-back clause does the persuading — do not stack more reasons on top of it.
    Ninety days on your own tenders is seven and a half thousand, and it comes off your first year in full if you carry on.
  4. 04
    Only discuss the annual plan once the pilot is agreed
    If asked earlier, give the range and move on. The whole point of the hybrid is that the renewal is priced from measured usage, so quoting a firm figure now throws away your best card.
    For a team of ten it has been landing between thirty and forty-five, but I would rather size it off what you actually run than guess at it today.

Why $7,500

It is not a cost-recovery number and it is not a discount. It is set just under the point where most large organisations require a competitive process to buy anything — so a single manager can approve it out of their own budget.

  • Check their threshold before the meeting. Delegated authority is commonly $10k or $25k. If theirs is lower, the pilot price moves down to fit under it — that matters far more than the revenue.
  • The irony is worth naming out loud. A procurement team should not have to run a procurement exercise to buy a procurement tool. It usually gets a laugh and it makes the point.
  • Fixed fee, not metered. A pilot invoiced by usage would need billing infrastructure that does not exist yet. Fixed fee sidesteps that entirely — do not offer per-tender pricing for a pilot.

Objections

"That is expensive for a trial."

Put it against one tender, not against software. Then hand the risk back to them.

It is about two days of one engineer's time, and it comes back to you if we go ahead. The question is whether thirty comparisons are worth two days — you will know inside the first week.

Do not discount here. A discount this early teaches them every number is soft, and the credit-back already removes the risk.

"How is usage actually measured? We do not want a meter running."

Lead with the cap, never with the mechanism. They are asking about risk, not about units.

You agree an annual allowance up front and that is the number on the invoice. If you are heading towards it we tell you before you reach it. There is nothing to reconcile at month end.

Never volunteer credits, tokens or per-document rates. If they press for the internal unit, say it is how we make sure a heavy user is not subsidised by a quiet one — that is a fairness answer, and it satisfies almost everyone.

"What if we run more tenders than the allowance?"

This is a buying signal, not a risk. Treat it as one.

Then we size the next year properly and top you up in the meantime — it is not a penalty, it just means the tool is doing what you hoped.
"Can we buy it outright, or host it ourselves?"

Do not refuse — it is usually a data question wearing a commercial costume. Answer the data question first.

Let me answer what is behind that first: it deploys inside your approved region, your documents are never training data, and every step is in an audit trail your compliance team can read. If a perpetual arrangement still matters after that, it is a conversation — but it is a different price and a different support model.
"We would need to run this through procurement."

That is exactly what the pilot price is designed to avoid. Say so directly.

That is why the pilot is priced where it is — it should sit inside your own signing authority. Worth checking the threshold before we make it harder than it needs to be.
"Can you do the pilot for free?"

No — and the reason is about them, not about us.

I would rather not. A free trial gets set up by whoever has a spare afternoon and then nobody owns it. A pilot someone signed for gets real tenders and a real answer in ninety days.

A free pilot has the worst conversion rate of any motion available to you. Hold this line.

Three things that must not leave this page

  • The $9,000 cost-plus tier. It prices a week of engineering at about twelve dollars a tender. Shown to a client it does not read as good value — it reads as an admission that the product is not worth much, and you can never raise it afterwards.
  • The per-tender cost to serve. Any client who learns a comparison costs us under three dollars will anchor there permanently, and no value argument survives it.
  • That usage metering does not exist yet. There is no token accounting in the product, which is precisely why the pilot is a fixed fee and the annual is an agreed allowance rather than a meter. Do not promise usage dashboards or itemised billing until that is built.

Before you quote a renewal

  • Count what they actually ran. Tenders, seats genuinely used, and — if they will tell you — hours saved per comparison. That is the renewal's whole basis.
  • Get one sentence from a user. A quote from the engineer who stopped reading bids by hand is worth more at renewal than any slide in the deck.
  • Price at roughly a tenth of the value you can evidence. Capturing much more invites a build-versus-buy review; capturing much less leaves you unable to fund support.
  • Never renew flat because it is easier. If usage grew, the price grows with it — that is the entire argument for having measured it.
